Working-Town Social Deduction — Game
Design Doc
Oct 4, 2026  ·  @Zain Asad
Elevator pitch
Six friends live and work in a small town for five days. Five are ordinary workers; one is
secretly covering up a crime while holding down the same kind of job as everyone else.
Every job controls something the criminal needs, so the impostor has to lie, trade, steal
and manipulate without looking suspicious. At the end of each day the town compares
stories and evidence, but innocent players have secrets of their own. Catch the criminal
before Day 5, or help them get away with it without realizing.
Core hook: one player runs a multi-step cover-up inside a functioning town, while
everyone else does legitimate jobs that naturally overlap with the resources the impostor
needs. Positioning: Among Us × Hitman-style sandbox × detective game, without combat.
Design pillars
These are the rules every system gets checked against. If a feature breaks one, cut or
rework it.
1. Every criminal action resembles at least one legitimate task. If the impostor carries a
suspicious bag, some jobs carry bags. If they need a tool, several jobs use it. If they
need a restricted room, at least one civilian task sometimes sends people there.
2. Evidence is never binary. Aim for "I saw Zain leave the pharmacy carrying something,"
not "I saw red kill." Every suspicious act should have a plausible innocent explanation.
3. Everyone has a real job, including the impostor. The impostor is never the one person
wandering aimlessly; their cover is built in.
4. Innocents lie too. Personal secrets give civilians reasons to act shifty, so lying is not
proof of guilt.
5. Work matters to the town. Ignoring your shift to stalk suspects hurts the civilian team.
The tension is "I think he's sus, but I still need to finish my shift."
6. Small, dense, systemic. Players should run into each other every couple of minutes.
"Why are YOU here?" is the target moment, not "Where is everyone?"

7. Crime is stylized. Evidence bags, suspicious packages, contaminated clothes, stolen
tools, forged records. Game-y and funny, not grim.
Round structure
Six players, five in-game days of 7–10 minutes each. Five are legitimate workers; one is
secretly the criminal, randomly assigned a normal public job like everyone else. Civilians
win by identifying the criminal; the criminal wins by completing the Day 5 cover-up or
escape.
Most deduction games loop task, meeting, task, meeting with no larger arc. Here each day
escalates: crime objectives get riskier, records start appearing on Day 3, and meeting
powers grow until the final accusation.
Task layers
Every player gets public job tasks plus a private personal task. The impostor gets both of
those plus hidden crime tasks, and must juggle all three.

Example personal secrets
Shopkeeper: make $500 today.
Mechanic: hide that you damaged a customer's car.
Doctor: you misplaced medication; recover it quietly.
Delivery driver: finish every delivery before closing.
The impostor's tradeoff: do the job well and look innocent, or spend time on the crime
and fall behind on work. Poor work performance shows up at meetings as circumstantial
evidence, never proof.
Crossover rule: innocent tasks must routinely send players to the back alley, pharmacy
storage, warehouse and garbage area. If only the impostor ever goes there, players solve
the meta in three rounds.
Jobs
Each profession is both a job and an information source. Avoid "press E three times to
mop the floor"; every job should carry a social responsibility.

Records should be partial: a time without a name, a jacket colour without a face, an item
without a buyer. The impostor can also target records (alter a log, forge a receipt) as a
crime task.
Town stability and the day report
Every completed job task feeds a shared Town Stability meter (or Revenue / Reputation).
If it collapses, civilians lose even if they caught the criminal. This stops the game
degenerating into six people stalking each other from Day 1.
At the end of each day, before the meeting, everyone sees a report like this:
DAY 2 REPORT
Town productivity: 82%
Mechanic       3/4 tasks
Shopkeeper     4/4
Sanitation     2/3
Doctor         4/4
Delivery       3/5
Construction   4/4
Suspicious events:
- Warehouse inventory discrepancy
- Unregistered vehicle movement
- Missing cleaning supplies
The report gives the group concrete things to argue about instead of pure vibes. A low
task count is a lead, not a verdict: the delivery driver may just have had five tasks.
Meetings and accusations
No daily ejection vote. With six players, Among Us-style voting collapses the round: one
random Day 1 vote and one wrong Day 2 vote leave four players. Instead, meeting powers
escalate.

Alternative to test: the town holds a single arrest warrant for the whole game. Either way,
a wrong accusation should hand the impostor a major advantage so nobody spams votes.
Impostor freedom and replayability
Each crime phase has several routes, so players can't memorize "construction yard =
impostor." A fixed list (go store, take knife, go warehouse) is solved after ten games.
Each route forces interaction with a different player's workplace. One round the criminal
keeps visiting sanitation; the next they never leave the mechanic's side.

No city. The demo is one tiny town block of about seven locations, dense enough that
players bump into each other constantly.

MVP scope
The first playable answers one question: do friends naturally start lying to each other? If
yes, expand.

Open questions to playtest
One accusation at the end
Escalating meeting powers, or one arrest warrant for the whole game?
What exactly does a wrong accusation give the impostor?
How low can Town Stability fall before civilians lose, and is that fun or frustrating?
Does the impostor win on completing all crime tasks, or only on surviving Day 5?
Are 7–10 minute days too long for 4 players on one street?
Do personal secrets create enough false suspicion without drowning the real signal?
Should players be able to lock resources or deny requests, and how does that affect
the Day 4 panic?
